# Replace Euler fault steps with cached maps

> **Note (2026-11-27).** This review was written on 2026-11-26, before the master fixes
> #427 to #429 removed the exponential cost of chains of cutting faults. After those fixes
> the cost is linear in the depth of a chain, so precomputed displacement maps are not part of
> spec 007 (see its "Out of scope"). The review is kept as background.

The literature supports the plan: five separate fields (flow-map visualization, semi-Lagrangian CFD, diffeomorphic image registration, sharp-interface numerics and geological fault modelling) converge on storing the *integrated end-point displacement* on a regular grid, applying it by trilinear lookup, and composing maps rather than re-integrating. Once a fault's map is built, the step count stops being a runtime cost: the map can be integrated with RK4 or many sub-steps at build time, and the runtime error becomes the trilinear interpolation error, which is second order in cell size (halving the spacing cuts it about 4x) ([Hlawatsch et al. 2011](https://vcg.iwr.uni-heidelberg.de/static/publications/hlawatsch2011hlic.pdf)). For LoopStructural this replaces the current cost, which grows about 21x per level of a cutting-fault chain, with one lookup per fault per point, so a chain costs a linear sum. No published source builds per-fault, per-side displacement grids with a hanging-wall/footwall mask, and none measures fault-operator error against step count or grid spacing, so those parts of the design rest on transferred theory rather than precedent. The discontinuity is the main risk: a single interpolated map across the fault smears the slip over one to two cells, as sliding-organ registration shows ([Pace et al. 2011](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dfpace/assets/publications/slidingOrgansUsingAnisotropicDiffusive.pdf)). The XFEM and ghost-fluid literature gives the fix: store each side's field *extended smoothly* across the surface and pick the side with the sign of the exact fault level set at the query point. Restore and deform should each have their own map, and the stored round-trip residual serves as the accuracy check.

## Ten Euler steps buy first-order accuracy at the highest cost

The current operator in `FaultSegment.apply_to_points` (`LoopStructural/modelling/features/fault/_fault_segment.py`) takes `steps=10` explicit Euler steps. Each step re-evaluates the three frame coordinates and the gradient of the slip coordinate at the moved points. Because an older fault's frame was built in space restored by younger faults, every one of those evaluations goes back through the younger faults, which is where the growth per chain level comes from. The papers that define the operator say only that non-planar faults are restored "using Euler's method, where the displacement is applied iteratively using small linear steps". They give no step count and no error analysis ([Grose et al. 2021b](https://gmd.copernicus.org/preprints/gmd-2021-112/gmd-2021-112-manuscript-version4.pdf)), and the only published timing is about 8 minutes for a whole model, with no breakdown for the fault operator ([Grose et al. 2021a](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/14/3915/2021/)). The search found no published study of fault-operator cost for large cross-cutting networks, so the exponential nesting looks like an implementation issue nobody has written up.

Ten Euler steps are also a poor accuracy buy. Forward Euler is first order. The semi-Lagrangian literature shows that one large step plus one correction is already second order. The MacCormack correction, applied to an ODE, is "identical to second order accurate Runge-Kutta". On a model problem, at the same step sizes, Euler error falls only from 6.6e-2 to 8.4e-3 (order 1.0), while MacCormack falls from 1.7e-2 to 1.8e-4 (order about 2.1) ([Selle et al. 2008](https://yingjie.math.gatech.edu/publications/SFKLR.pdf)). Atmospheric models compute departure points with a midpoint-rule trajectory solved by fixed-point iteration, where "two iterations are enough to obtain satisfactory (second order) accuracy". The iteration converges only if the time step times the maximum velocity shear is below 1, that is, if trajectories do not cross ([Diamantakis 2013](https://www.ecmwf.int/sites/default/files/elibrary/2014/9054-semi-lagrangian-technique-atmospheric-modelling-current-status-and-future-challenges.pdf); [CAPS CFD notes](https://twister.caps.ou.edu/CFD2023/Chapter6.pdf)). Applied to a point (my algebra, consistent with the Selle result), "Euler step, step back, correct by half the residual" reduces to Heun's method: x + (h/2)(v(x) + v(x + h·v(x))). Two or three Heun steps (4 to 6 field evaluations) would beat ten Euler steps (10 evaluations) wherever slip times the field's curvature is modest. Near fault tips and strongly curved surfaces the condition can fail, and there Stam's practice of sub-stepping "only in regions of high velocity gradients" is the precedent ([Stam 1999](https://cgweb.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/intern/seminar/gridFluids_StableFluids.pdf)).

That analysis matters for two reasons even if the grid map goes ahead. Heun is a cheap fallback that cuts the current runtime cost by 2 to 5x with no new data structure. It is also the right integrator for *building* the map, although at build time cost is cheap enough that RK4 with many sub-steps is the better choice.

## Precomputed maps trade integration error for O(h²) interpolation error

Hierarchical line integration stores a "coordinate map" φ(x) at every node of a fixed-resolution grid. It builds longer maps by self-composition with trilinear lookups, so cost becomes logarithmic in integration length. The global error after n composition levels is bounded by roughly (c²M/2L)·e^{nL}, where c is the cell size, M the maximum second derivative of the map and L its Lipschitz constant. The error is therefore "second order in the cell size", and it concentrates where the flow stretches (high FTLE). The 95th-percentile endpoint errors on 2D test flows were 0.001 to 0.1% of the domain ([Hlawatsch et al. 2011](https://vcg.iwr.uni-heidelberg.de/static/publications/hlawatsch2011hlic.pdf)). The same paper makes two practical recommendations that carry over directly. First, store *relative* displacements (end minus start) rather than absolute positions, to avoid cancellation. Second, do the trilinear interpolation in software rather than with GPU texture hardware, which uses 9-bit weights. Storing displacement has a further benefit for faults: the zero-displacement region far from the fault is reproduced exactly.

Registration gives the most concrete numbers. Scaling and squaring takes one Euler step x + v/2^K and composes the result with itself K times, so 2^K Euler steps cost only K grid compositions. In single precision the optimum is K of about 6 to 7 (64 to 128 equivalent steps). More squarings make the result worse, because resampling and floating-point error dominate ([Ashburner 2007](https://users.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/~jesper/papers/readgroup_071009/Ashburner07.pdf)). ITK chooses K automatically so that the first step is under 0.5 pixel: N = ceil(2 + 0.5·log2(max|v|²/spacing²)) ([ITK source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/InsightSoftwareConsortium/ITK/master/Modules/Filtering/DisplacementField/include/itkExponentialDisplacementFieldImageFilter.hxx)). A benchmark of exponential algorithms found scaling and squaring the best trade-off, but with an *intrinsic lower bound in accuracy* that only grid oversampling raises ([Bossa, Zacur, Olmos 2008](https://mlanthology.org/cvprw/2008/bossa2008cvprw-algorithms)). The lesson for faults is that once a map exists, the grid spacing sets the accuracy, not the step count.

Post hoc Lagrangian flow analysis is structurally the same as the fault proposal: end positions stored on a uniform grid, interpolated for new particles, and chained interval by interval. With the full basis it was "over fifteen times more accurate" than Eulerian advection, and it stayed more accurate at 1/16 of the storage. Its authors also report that one long trajectory is more accurate than a chain of short ones, because interpolation error accumulates over the chain ([Agranovsky et al. 2014](https://vis.cs.ucdavis.edu/vis2014papers/VIS_Conference/ldav/papers/agranovsky.pdf)). That favours one finely integrated map per fault over a stack of partial maps.

LoopStructural applies each fault's map once, not 2^K times, so the exponential amplification term hardly applies. Each map contributes about (h²/8)·max|∂²d| of trilinear error (standard interpolation theory, not from these sources). That error is largest where the displacement profile varies fastest: at fault tips, in the far-field decay of D0, and on curved fault surfaces. Whether this beats the current scheme depends on the cell size relative to those length scales, and no source measures it for fault fields. A numerical comparison against a high-step-count reference is the first thing to run.

## Compose by sequential lookup, not by regridding the chain

There are two ways to compose maps along a cutting chain, and the literature points to one of them.

| Option | Runtime cost per point | Resampling error | Discontinuity handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current: 10 Euler steps, nested restoration | ~21x per chain level (exponential in depth) | none (first-order integration error instead) | exact side test each step |
| A: apply per-fault maps in sequence (youngest first) | one trilinear lookup + one side test per fault (linear in chain length) | one interpolation per fault | exact side test per fault |
| B: pre-compose the whole chain into one grid | one lookup | n−1 extra resamplings | every younger fault's jump is baked into the older map and smeared |

Composition on a grid means u_AB(x) = u_A(x) + u_B(x + u_A(x)), and every composition adds one resampling error. DARTEL notes that recursively composed quantities are "only an approximation because of the effect of iteratively resampling" ([Ashburner 2007](https://users.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/~jesper/papers/readgroup_071009/Ashburner07.pdf)). Option B has a worse problem for faults: the composed field is discontinuous across *every* fault in the chain, so a per-side representation no longer works. Option A keeps each map smooth on its own side and keeps the fault topology, which seismic "all faults at once" unfaulting gives up ([Grose et al. 2021b](https://gmd.copernicus.org/preprints/gmd-2021-112/gmd-2021-112-manuscript-version4.pdf)). Option A is also cheap enough that B's saving does not justify its error.

Option A fits LoopStructural's coordinate convention, but only if each map lives in the right space. An older fault's frame is built in the space restored by its younger faults, and the code already supports evaluating it there without re-applying them (`restored_through`). The map for fault k should therefore be sampled on grid nodes in *its own frame's space* (restored through the younger faults). It is then queried with the point as it arrives from the younger faults' maps. Building each map costs one frame evaluation per node per integration sub-step, with no nested restoration. The total build cost becomes linear in the number of faults. One open point the literature cannot settle: the frame evaluation also depends on the `block` (which side of each younger fault). If that changes the map, a map key is (fault, own side, block of the younger faults). Only block combinations that actually occur in the model should be built, otherwise the count grows combinatorially.

## The jump belongs in the side test, never in the interpolant

Interpolating one field across a sliding interface fails in a well-documented way: "the opposing vectors near the boundary will be averaged and the motion at the interface will not be recovered" ([Pace et al. 2011](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dfpace/assets/publications/slidingOrgansUsingAnisotropicDiffusive.pdf)). Registration accepts a transition zone of 1 to 2 voxels and is only as accurate as the boundary segmentation ([Pace et al. 2013](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dfpace/assets/publications/slidingRegistration.pdf)). Piecewise-diffeomorphic registration keeps maps that are not smooth across the interface yet still almost always invertible ([Risser et al. 2013](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/piecewise-diffeomorphic-image-registration-application-to-the-mot/)). Precomputed fault fields can do better than either, because the fault surface is known exactly as the zero level set of the frame coordinate gx.

XFEM puts faults and cracks on non-conforming meshes by adding a Heaviside function H times extra degrees of freedom to the nodes whose support the surface cuts. The side is taken from the sign of a level set, so the jump is exact. This has been applied directly to reservoir faults on structured and unstructured meshes ([Moës et al. 1999](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/entities/publication/6b4f2ef5-581a-4106-986b-e110834db063); [Stolarska et al. 2001](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01007079); [Prevost & Sukumar 2015](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/multi-scale-x-fem-faults-simulations-for-reservoir-geomechanical-/)). The ghost fluid method does the same on fixed grids: each side gets extrapolated "ghost" values on the other side of the interface, so every stencil sees only one side's data ([Fedkiw et al. 1999](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/6369092)). PDE extrapolation fills those ghost values to constant, linear or quadratic order ([Aslam 2004](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1466580)). Extrapolating Cartesian derivatives keeps second- and third-order L∞ accuracy even at kinks and high curvature ([Bochkov & Gibou 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09559)).

Translated to the proposal, the stored field is u(x) = H(gx(x))·u_hw(x) + (1 − H(gx(x)))·u_fw(x). Here u_hw and u_fw are each extended across the fault, and H is evaluated from the exact gx at the query point, never interpolated. Three consequences follow, all inferred from these sources rather than measured. First, the extension must cover every node of every cell the fault cuts, which is one cell beyond the surface for trilinear lookup; two or three cells add a safety margin. Second, a one-sided field stored with its cutoff (for example D0 = 0 for f0 < 0) will smear within one cell. LoopStructural already has the right extension: `block_displacement` evaluates the smooth continuation of a side's profile across the fault, so the extended values come analytically, with no PDE. Third, the residual error is in *where the jump sits*. If gx itself came from a coarse interpolated copy, points in a thin sliver would get the wrong side and an error the size of the full slip. The side test should use the model's own gx.

In LoopStructural the footwall does not move, so u_fw = 0 and only the hanging-wall field needs storing, as in u = H(gx)·u_hw. That halves memory compared with two full grids. The semi-Lagrangian limiter literature adds a useful rule: correction schemes "automatically revert to the first order accurate scheme when the upwind and downwind building blocks pull data from non-commensurate regions" ([Selle et al. 2008](https://yingjie.math.gatech.edu/publications/SFKLR.pdf)). The fault analogue: if a point's restored position lands on the other side of a fault it should not cross, or in a different block, flag it and fall back to direct integration. None of the semi-Lagrangian sources treats a discontinuous *velocity* field, so the theory behind those schemes only holds within one side.

## Build restore and deform as separate maps, and check the round trip

Every field surveyed obtains the inverse by integrating the negated field and storing a second map, not by inverting the forward map. Hierarchical integration keeps separate forward and backward hierarchies ([Hlawatsch et al. 2011](https://vcg.iwr.uni-heidelberg.de/static/publications/hlawatsch2011hlic.pdf)), and ITK's `ComputeInverse` simply divides the field by −2^N ([ITK source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/InsightSoftwareConsortium/ITK/master/Modules/Filtering/DisplacementField/include/itkExponentialDisplacementFieldImageFilter.hxx)). The accuracy gap is large. With K = 6, exp(v)∘exp(−v) deviated from identity by 0.023 voxel RMS and at most 0.40 voxel. The naive inverse that subtracts the displacement gave 0.15 to 0.17 voxel RMS and up to 4.0 voxels, about 7x worse in RMS and 10x worse at the maximum ([Ashburner 2007](https://users.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/~jesper/papers/readgroup_071009/Ashburner07.pdf)). Negating a stored displacement "is oversimplified and can cause large errors for large deformations or deformations that are composites of several deformations" ([Chen et al. 2008](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-simple-fixed-point-approach-to-invert-a-deformation-field/)).

There are two sound routes to the inverse. The first integrates the negated field (the current `reverse=True` path) into its own grid, at the same cost as the forward map. The second inverts the stored forward map with the Chen fixed-point iteration v_n(x) = −u(x + v_{n−1}(x)), which typically converges in under ten iterations, each one a trilinear lookup ([Chen et al. 2008](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-simple-fixed-point-approach-to-invert-a-deformation-field/)). ITK's damped version stops at 0.1 voxel maximum or 0.001 voxel mean error, within 20 iterations ([ITK InvertDisplacementFieldImageFilter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/InsightSoftwareConsortium/ITK/master/Modules/Filtering/DisplacementField/include/itkInvertDisplacementFieldImageFilter.hxx)). The two routes answer different questions. The negated-field map is the better approximation of the true continuous inverse. The fixed-point inverse is consistent with the *discrete* forward map, which matters when a model restores data, interpolates, and then deforms again, as in the "unfault-and-refault" workflow ([Godefroy et al. 2017, via Grose et al. 2021b](https://gmd.copernicus.org/preprints/gmd-2021-112/gmd-2021-112-manuscript-version4.pdf)). For fault slip, which is close to a rigid translation along the surface, the two should agree closely. Brunton and Rowley's bidirectional map composition fails mainly where the map strongly compresses or stretches ([Brunton & Rowley 2010](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/fast-computation-of-finite-time-lyapunov-exponent-fields-for-unst/)), which mostly excludes fault interiors but not tips. For inverses, the side test runs on the deformed-space level set, so the inverse map needs its own side mask in deformed coordinates.

The round trip is also the cheapest error indicator. The UFLOW check traces forward, then back, and compares ([McLoughlin et al. 2010](https://people.cs.nott.ac.uk/pszrsl/research/star/geometric/mcloughlin10over.pdf)). Registration reports inverse-consistency error as the RMS and maximum of Φ∘Φ⁻¹ − identity. Jacobians compose by the chain rule, so a non-positive determinant flags folding ([Ashburner 2007](https://users.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/~jesper/papers/readgroup_071009/Ashburner07.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The literature changes the question from "how many Euler steps?" to "what grid spacing, and where?". Once maps are cached, integration accuracy is nearly free and interpolation accuracy is what is bought. That spending belongs near fault tips, in the far-field decay and on curved surfaces, not at the fault plane, because an exact side test removes the jump from the interpolant altogether. The design that follows is this.

Store one map per fault, as displacement rather than position. Sample it in that fault's own frame space (restored through the younger faults). Build it at load time with RK4 or Heun sub-steps, using `block_displacement` to fill hanging-wall values smoothly at least two cells into the footwall. Keep the footwall implicit as zero. Choose the spacing so that the profile's second derivatives are resolved, with the ITK half-cell rule on the build sub-steps as a floor. At query time, apply the maps youngest first, one trilinear lookup each, gated by H of the exact gx. Never pre-compose a chain onto one grid. Build the restore map by integrating the negated field, and keep or derive a fixed-point-refined inverse wherever restore-then-deform consistency matters. Store the max and RMS round-trip residual and the minimum Jacobian determinant with each map. Fall back to direct Heun integration for points that cross blocks, leave the grid, or sit where the residual exceeds tolerance, using a border flag in the style of Hlawatsch for points that leave the domain. One gap must be closed before committing: no source quantifies this error for fault-like fields. A test of grid spacing against a 1000-step reference on a curved, tip-bounded fault is the first experiment, and it also gives the default spacing.
